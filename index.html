01 Final Feedback to Incorporate

Final Hi-Fi user testing and TA feedback surfaced two areas for improvement — one around visual consistency and one around interaction clarity.

Issue 01
Navigation bars were inconsistent across feature screens. The study group flow used a different nav structure than the rest of the prototype, making it feel disconnected from the overall LeetCode redesign. Users noted that switching between features felt jarring.
Issue 02
Scaffolding request pop-ups lacked visual polish. The modal for posting a scaffolding request to the group didn't match the styling of other pop-ups in the prototype and felt like a placeholder rather than a finished interaction.
Changes Made
Unified Navigation
Synced up the navigation bar across all study group screens to match the shared prototype nav used by the other four features, ensuring a consistent experience when moving between sections.
Scaffolding Request Polish
Updated the scaffolding request pop-up to use the same modal styling, spacing, and button patterns as other confirmation dialogs in the prototype for a more cohesive look and feel.
02 Reflection on the Assignment
What Went Well

The contextual inquiry and user testing phases were by far the most valuable parts of the project. Watching real CS students interact with LeetCode — and hearing them say things like "I'm not going to help these other people, I just want to grind" — gave us signal we never would have gotten from surveys or assumptions. The study group feature went through the most dramatic evolution of any feature in our group precisely because we kept testing with real users and listening to what they actually said rather than what we expected. The affinity diagramming process also helped us see that "limited social and motivational support structures" was a real theme, but that users wanted competition, not collaboration, to fill that gap.

What Went Badly

The initial design was too idealistic. I built the study group concept around Vygotsky's ZPD and sociocultural learning theory — peer scaffolding, mentor roles, collaborative problem-solving — but every round of user testing pushed back on this framing. Users didn't want to wait for strangers to respond. They didn't want to be mentors without strong incentives. They didn't want their progress visible to a group. I should have validated the collaboration assumption earlier before investing in the full matching and karma systems. The pivot to competition was the right call but it came late.

What Felt Unnecessary or Needed More Time

The mid-fi stage felt like a checkbox exercise for this feature — basically a cleaner version of the low-fi sketches that didn't surface meaningfully different feedback. The hi-fi user testing desperately needed more time. We only tested with five people total across all five features, and more diverse testers would have given us a fuller picture of whether the competitive pivot works for everyone or just for confident grinders.

How I Would Change the Assignment

I would have started with a simple "would you actually use this?" validation before building any prototype. A 5-minute conversation asking CS students "would you join an online study group with strangers to practice LeetCode?" would have immediately surfaced the resistance that took us three prototype rounds to discover. I'd also allocate more time to hi-fi testing and less to mid-fi, and add a structured checkpoint where each feature has to justify its learning-science grounding against what real users are actually saying.

04 Group Project — Study Groups
Study Groups
Matched peer groups that evolved from collaboration to competition
self-determination-theory vicarious-experiences mastery-goals
4A · Background & Pain Points
Problem space

LeetCode is the default practice platform for CS students preparing for technical interviews. But it treats problem-solving as an entirely solitary activity. There are no built-in mechanisms for peer support, no way to see that others are struggling with the same problems, and no social accountability beyond public stats that often create more anxiety than motivation.

Target users

CS undergraduates and early-career engineers preparing for internship and full-time interviews who feel isolated during the grind — particularly those whose motivation drops when studying alone for extended periods.

Performance Anxiety & Discouragement
Difficulty labels create unhealthy comparison; failure feels like a referendum on intelligence rather than a learning gap; progress is framed as "can you solve it or not" rather than skill development.
Limited Social & Motivational Support Structures
Studying alone feels isolating and demotivating; LeetCode doesn't provide collaboration opportunities; users lack reminders of the value and progress of their work.
Motivation Tied Only to Interviews
Users only open LeetCode when an interview is approaching; outside of interview season the app feels irrelevant; daily practice is not a natural motivator.

Study Groups targets the second cluster directly — the absence of social and motivational structures that could sustain engagement beyond solo grinding.

4B · UX Research
Contextual inquiry

We observed CS students at varying skill levels attempting LeetCode problems in their natural study environments. We paid particular attention to what happened when they got stuck: did they reach out to anyone? How did isolation affect their willingness to continue?

Students who studied alone were significantly more likely to disengage after repeated failures. Multiple participants mentioned wanting to know if others were struggling with the same problems, but LeetCode offered no way to see this without exposing themselves to competitive pressure.

"I'd love to know someone else is stuck on this too. But I don't want them seeing my fail count."
— Participant, contextual inquiry
Affinity diagramming

We grouped raw observations into sticky notes, clustered them under shared themes, and identified parent categories. "Limited social and motivational support structures" emerged as a major theme with sub-clusters around isolation, lack of collaboration, and the absence of progress reminders. This validated the study group concept while foreshadowing the tension between wanting connection and fearing exposure.

4C · Design Inspirations

Study Groups is grounded in learning-science principles drawn from the course readings:

How People Learn · Chapter 6
Self-Determination Theory — Relatedness
Behavior is driven by three needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Study groups address relatedness by reducing isolation — users see others facing the same struggles, sustaining motivation through social connection rather than solitary grinding.
ABCs of How We Learn · "Yes I Can" (Bandura)
Vicarious Experiences & Social Persuasion
Self-efficacy is built through mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, social persuasion, and physiological signals. Seeing peers at a similar level succeed builds self-efficacy without toxic comparison, and receiving encouragement from a mentor provides social persuasion that effort leads to improvement.
How Learning Works · Chapter 3
Mastery over Performance Goals
Performance goals can hurt motivation when they become performance-avoidant. The group structure shifts focus from individual rankings to collective skill-building, encouraging mastery-oriented goals that sustain engagement over time.

Pattern inspirations: Reddit's karma and community model, chess Elo rating systems, Discord study servers, and Type Racer's head-to-head competitive format.

4D · Low- & Mid-Fidelity Prototyping

Four rounds of prototype testing each surfaced new issues and dramatically reshaped the feature:

How ideas evolved

The biggest conceptual shift across fidelities was moving from "groups as collaboration" to "groups as competition." The low-fi prototype assumed users wanted peer support — matched strangers working through problems together with a mentor guiding the way. But every round of testing pushed back. Users didn't want to wait for strangers. They didn't want to expose their struggles. What they did want was to see where they stood relative to peers, race friends on timed problems, and track improvement through a concrete rating. By hi-fi, the feature had pivoted from a collaborative study group to a competitive practice arena with optional peer support.

4E · User Testing & Final Prototype
Hi-fi testing

We ran contextual inquiry on the Hi-Fi Figma prototype with five users testing all five features. For study groups, we walked users through the full flow: discovering and joining a group, viewing the group lobby, posting a scaffolding request, and interacting with group members.

What we learned

The most direct feedback came from a user who said he wouldn't help strangers and would only use the feature for tracking where he should be. Another said they would never join an online study group but would "send a friend a LeetCode link and race them." A third suggested making it "like chess Elo — you just want to see your number go up." Across all testers, the social awareness of the feature was valuable (knowing others exist, seeing relative progress), but the collaborative mechanics were not.

Final iteration

We added a competitive practice mode with timed head-to-head challenges, reworked karma into an Elo-style skill rating, and reframed the group experience to lead with competition. The collaborative scaffolding request was preserved but moved to a secondary position.

05 Individual Project — Habla
Habla
A Spanish micro-learning app teaching vocabulary through bite-sized lessons and quizzes
micro-learning quiz-checkpointed beginner-friendly
5A · Design Rationale
Chosen topic and why

I chose Spanish vocabulary because it's a universally practical skill that lends itself well to the micro-learning format. Vocabulary acquisition is inherently incremental — words can be grouped into thematic categories like greetings, numbers, and food & drink — making it natural to break into short, self-contained lessons. Even learning 20 common words unlocks basic communication in real-world situations, giving the app a clear and immediate value proposition.

Main user flow

The app follows an eight-screen flow. The user opens the Home screen and selects a lesson category (Greetings, Numbers, Food & Drink, Emotions). They see a Lesson Detail screen explaining what they'll learn, then move into Lesson Content with Spanish-English word pairs. After reading, they take a three-question Quiz with multiple-choice answers. The Feedback screen shows correct/incorrect results immediately. A Progress screen tracks completion across categories, and a Completion screen confirms the lesson is done before returning Home with updated checkmarks.

Key design decisions

Layout: Consistent lesson cards with a warm color palette (reds and creams) that feels inviting rather than clinical. Each screen has one clear primary action to keep navigation intuitive. Hierarchy: The flow is deliberately linear — Home → Detail → Content → Quiz → Feedback → Progress → Completion — so users always know where they are. Interactions: In the quiz, correct answers are highlighted in green with a checkmark. If an answer is wrong, the app shows which was correct and lets the user continue without penalty, keeping the experience low-stakes and encouraging learning from mistakes.

What I learned while building the prototype

The biggest lesson was how much small interaction details matter in a micro-learning context. Showing the correct answer after a wrong quiz response — rather than just saying "wrong, try again" — completely changed the emotional tone from punitive to supportive. I also learned that keeping lessons genuinely short is harder than it sounds; the temptation is always to add more content, but the constraint of brevity is what makes the format work. Finally, building branching states in Figma (correct vs. incorrect vs. completion) required far more frames than I expected.
